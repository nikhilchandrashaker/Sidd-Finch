An analytics and biomechanics project on baseball’s most famous nonexistent pitcher, the New York Mets prospect who threw 168 mph in George Plimpton’s 1985 Sports Illustrated April Fools’ story.

Scouting report

In the story, Finch is a Mets camp arrival who learned his craft in Tibet, studied at Harvard, plays the French horn, and pitches wearing a single boot. His fastball is clocked at 168 mph with pinpoint control, and he is undecided about whether to play at all. None of it is real, so every number here is a modeled estimate built to ask one question: what would 168 mph demand of a human body?

2. Biomechanics: how a body makes 168 mph

A fastball is built by the kinetic chain: legs and hips start the motion, the trunk rotates, and each segment hands speed to the next until the hand releases the ball. Each slider scales one link’s contribution. The 94 mph baseline split is a simplified teaching model, not measured data.

Why it is hard

Kinetic energy rises with the square of speed, so 168 mph carries about 3.2 times the energy of a 94 mph pitch. Every link must add speed, and the final links are small, light muscles and tendons.

The ligament problem

Published lab studies put elite pitchers’ elbow torque in the range of 60 to 70 N·m, above the roughly 30 to 35 N·m the ulnar collateral ligament can take alone, with muscle carrying the rest. This page scales torque with speed squared, a crude assumption, so a true 168 mph arm would need a very different structure.

Method and limits

Flight time is distance from release (60.5 ft minus extension) divided by speed, ignoring drag, which would slow a real pitch by roughly 8 to 10 percent. Swing-and-miss rate is a logistic curve on the gap between hitter reaction time and the time left after a 150 ms swing, tuned to a 22 percent miss rate on a 94 mph fastball. The kinetic chain split, the torque scaling, and the game simulator are teaching models. Nothing here is a prediction, because Sidd Finch is fiction.
